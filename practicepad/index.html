The nudge ladder

If you hear one of these, your current code has a specific problem. Open a nudge only when you'd have earned it.

How round 1 actually runs

  • 45–60 minutes on Zoom with a shared pad. Usually one problem that grows in parts, not several problems.
  • Interns get class-building and implementation problems with an emphasis on clean code, not algorithm trivia. Jane Street says their questions are open-ended, with several reasonable approaches.
  • Interviewers may stay quiet. Silence is not a verdict. Keep narrating.
  • Hints are part of the format. Taking a nudge and running with it is a good signal, not a lost point.
  • They don't need you to finish. They want to watch you work.

Signal decoder

"This function is going to be called a lot."Cache it. Move the work to the rarer operations. Then say the new costs out loud.
"What if these overlap?"A set loses information. Count how many things reference each item.
"Do you need to recompute all of that?"Update incrementally from the change instead of rebuilding.
"How big can this get?"Your current approach won't scale. Say its cost in the problem's own sizes, then find what's actually small.
"What's the complexity of that?"Answer in the problem's real sizes, not a bare n.
"Is there a simpler way?"You're over-engineering. Step back and say the simplest thing that works.
"Does that handle…?"There's a bug. Trace their example through your code, line by line, out loud.
"How confident are you in that?"They want calibration: "Fairly sure about X, not sure about Y, let me check Y with an example."
"Would you play this game?"Expected value first, then variance and what's at stake. Ask what you're risking.

What gets graded

Paraphrased from Jane Street's own prep page and interviewing blog post. The one thing they repeat: the main question is whether they'd want to work with you, and the interview is a simulation of working with you.
  • Be nice. Pleasant and collaborative. They say plainly that brilliance doesn't get you hired if you aren't pleasant to work with.
  • Be clear. Simple and to the point. Use examples. Don't use a term you can't define on the spot.
  • Know your language. Write real, idiomatic code without looking things up: loops, classes, maps, exceptions, sensible organization.
  • Know what you don't know. Say "I'm not sure" rather than guess. State how confident you are in each assumption.
  • Write the code you said you'd write. Agree on a plan, then carry it out. Changing plans mid-way without discussing it is a named failure mode.
  • Name tradeoffs and pick a side, with a reason.
  • Ask thoughtful questions about unclear parts.
  • Engage with hints. Update on what the interviewer tells you.
The failure mode this pad is built around. The code in these problems is short for everyone who passes. Getting from a slow version to a fast one is expected, not the differentiator. The differentiator is whether the interviewer could follow your reasoning the whole time without asking. Every time you change the design, say three things: what was wrong, what the new invariant is, and what it costs now.

The 45-minute shape

0:00–4:00Restate the problem in your own words. Work a tiny example by hand. Ask 3–4 clarifying questions, including sizes and how often each operation runs. 4:00–8:00Plan out loud: what you'll store, what each function does, rough cost. Get a nod before typing. 8:00–22:00Part 1. Small named functions. Narrate as you go. Then trace one example through the code by hand. 22:00–34:00Part 2. Explain the change before making it: what breaks, the new idea, the new cost. 34:00–42:00Part 3. Same pattern. Reuse Part 1–2 pieces rather than rewriting. 42:00–45:00Part 4 discussion, then your questions for them.

Sentences that do the work

When you change the design

"The problem with what I have is that it redoes the whole computation on every call, so it's O(size of everything) even when nothing changed."

"So the invariant I'll keep is …. The rare operations maintain it; the hot one just reads it."

"That moves the cost to the rare operations. Given the call frequencies you gave me, that's the right trade."

When you're unsure

"I'm fairly confident about the main loop. I'm less sure about this edge case; let me check it against a small example before I commit."

When you take a hint

"Right, that case breaks my current approach because … I'll change just this piece; everything else stays the same."
